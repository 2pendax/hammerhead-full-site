Carton Flow Rack Planning and Testing Guide

Carton flow uses gravity to move cases, totes, or bins from a replenishment side toward a pick face. It can separate replenishment from picking and support first-in/first-out presentation, but only when containers and lanes behave reliably.

Short answer: profile each container, test its full operating range on the proposed lane, set pitch and guides, control speed and stops, design the pick face, separate replenishment traffic, and maintain the system. This page does not guarantee faster picks, improved accuracy, or nationwide design, installation, and permitting.

Container data determines the lane

Dimensions

Minimum and maximum width, depth, height, bottom contact area, feet, ribs, lips, taper, bulge, lids, handles and dimensional variation.

Weight range

Empty, minimum and maximum working weight, center of gravity, uneven contents, shifting products, partial cases and seasonal changes.

Material and condition

Corrugated board, plastic tote, bin or tray; stiffness; moisture; dust; tape; staples; crushed corners; bowing; wear and damaged-container rules.

Inventory behavior

SKU velocity, quantity, first-in/first-out need, lot or expiry, replenishment, pick unit, stockouts, substitutions and peak conditions.

Human interaction

Pick reach, presentation angle, label and barcode visibility, grasp, opening cartons, extracting items, empty removal and repetitive work.

Environment

Ambient, cold, freezer, humidity, dust, washdown, food or clean-area needs, corrosion, lubricants, debris and temperature-dependent behavior.

Lane elements to verify

ElementPurposeValidation questions
Wheels or rollersSupport and move the containerDoes contact spacing suit the bottom? Can small, soft, ribbed or damaged containers bridge, sink or track poorly?
PitchProvides gravity force toward the pick faceWill the lightest container advance and the heaviest arrive without excessive speed or impact?
Guides and dividersKeep containers aligned and lanes separatedDo tolerances, bulging, labels, hands and replenishment allow movement without binding or snagging?
Stops and presentationRetain the load and position it for pickingCan the stop withstand repeated impact and allow safe ergonomic access without product damage?
Brakes or speed controlManage speed in suitable applicationsAre devices compatible with the weight range, container contact, lane length, environment and maintenance plan?
Structure and supportsCarry lane and inventory loadsAre levels, beams, frames, connections, anchors, total loads and configuration documented for the exact system?

A successful test uses the extremes, not the average. Test the lightest and heaviest, smallest and largest, best and realistically worn containers under relevant temperature, fill, lane length and replenishment conditions. Record the approved container envelope and rejection criteria.

Planning and test sequence

Segment the SKU and container population

Group only containers that share support, tracking, weight, speed and presentation behavior. Assign unsuitable or highly variable items to another storage method.

Prototype representative lanes

Use proposed wheels or rollers, pitch, guides, stops, lane depth and pick face. Test replenishment, full travel, picking, restarts, jams and damaged containers.

Design the work zone

Coordinate pick heights, reach, labels, lighting, empty removal, carts, conveyors, replenishment aisle, lift equipment, pedestrian separation and stock visibility.

Coordinate structure and building

Review rack loads, anchors, slab, storage height, commodity, sprinklers, flue spaces, egress, environment, permits and integration with conveyors or pick modules.

Commission and control changes

Inspect installation, load only approved containers, train replenishment and picking, establish cleaning and maintenance, monitor jams and approve SKU or package changes.

Picking and replenishment must work together

Forward-pick capacity should be based on demand between reliable replenishments, not a desire to place every SKU in flow rack. Oversized pick faces consume length and can increase walking. Undersized faces create constant replenishment and stockouts. Use real order and replenishment data to size each lane assignment.

Separate lift-truck replenishment from people where possible, and define replenishment windows, location confirmation, lot logic, maximum lane load, container orientation, overfill prevention, empty removal and response to a stopped container. Never push a jam from an unsafe position or climb the rack.

Operational review questions

Advancement

Does every approved container move after the front unit is removed, including low weight, cold, worn and partially deformed examples?

Arrival

Can the heaviest load reach the stop without unsafe speed, bounce, product damage, excessive noise or stop overload?

Ergonomics

Are reach, height, wrist posture, grasp, carton opening, item extraction, label visibility and empty handling reviewed for the actual work?

Accuracy

Do location labels, dividers, scan rules, replenishment confirmation and exception procedures reduce—not merely relocate—error risk?

Maintenance

Can debris be removed and wheels, rollers, guides, stops, brakes, tracks and structure be inspected and replaced safely?

Change control

Who tests and approves new cartons, totes, weights, packaging, lane assignments, pitch, dividers, stops and environment?

Information for a carton flow brief

Provide container samples and specifications, full weight range, bottom geometry, condition limits, SKU and order data, replenishment frequency, lot or expiry rules, environment, pick methods, conveyors or carts, rack and building information, fire documents, schedule, and responsible reviewers.

Require the proposal to state the approved container envelope, lane test method, pitch, tracks, guides, stops, speed control, load basis, pick presentation, replenishment rules, maintenance, spare parts, fire assumptions, installation and acceptance tests. Performance should be demonstrated against agreed conditions.

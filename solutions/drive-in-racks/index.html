Density requires lane discipline

Drive-In Pallet Rack Planning Guide

Drive-in rack stores pallets on continuous side rails while a lift truck enters the lane to place or retrieve loads. It removes many access aisles, but trades selectivity for depth and places equipment inside the rack structure.

Short answer: use drive-in or drive-through rack only when SKU quantities, rotation, pallet quality, load consistency, equipment, lane discipline, fire conditions, and operating controls support it. This page does not promise 75% more capacity or nationwide design, engineering, and installation.

Test whether inventory fits deep lanes

SKU depth

Calculate pallet quantity by SKU, lot, date, status and temperature. A deep lane loses value when several products must share it or partial lanes remain common.

Rotation

Define last-in/first-out, first-in/first-out, lot, expiry and dispatch rules. Drive-through access can support different flow but does not automatically enforce rotation.

Demand variability

Model normal, peak, seasonal, promotion and declining inventory. Include the space effect of partly filled lanes and stranded pallets.

Pallet consistency

Verify pallet type, width, depth, underside, stringers or blocks, condition, deflection, repairs, overhang and compatibility with support rails.

Unit loads

Document maximum weight, height, width, depth, stability, center of gravity, wrap, product shift, commodity and damaged-load criteria.

Throughput

Measure puts and retrievals by hour, lane, SKU and peak. Include travel into lanes, blocked access, moves, counting, exceptions and replenishment.

Drive-in and drive-through differences

DecisionDrive-inDrive-through
AccessLane is normally entered from one faceLane can be accessed from opposite faces when layout and operation permit
Typical rotation logicOften last-in/first-out within a laneCan support first-in/first-out flow if receipt and retrieval sides remain controlled
Building footprintCan sit against an approved boundary while preserving required separation and accessRequires operating access and aisle conditions at both ends
Structure and protectionBack and side stability, truck entry and rear load control require attentionOpen access at both ends changes bracing, traffic, protection and operational controls
Use decisionMust follow actual SKU, pallet, load, equipment, fire, building and engineering requirements—not the desired density alone

Nominal density is not usable capacity. Compare actual pallets stored after lane assignment, partial lanes, incompatible SKUs, access, clearances, fire requirements, damage controls and operational reserves. A percentage borrowed from another warehouse is not a project result.

Lane and equipment design

Define the pallet envelope

Set approved pallet types, dimensions, maximum weight, underside support, condition, overhang, load stability and rejection rules. Test representative pallets on the proposed rails.

Coordinate the lift truck

Verify model, capacity at height and load center, mast, overhead guard, carriage, forks, guidance if used, lowered and raised clearances, turning and visibility.

Set lane geometry

Coordinate rail spacing and height, pallet clearance, bay and frame geometry, entry, floor, base, bracing, stops, guides, vertical clearance and placement tolerance.

Review building and fire interfaces

Assess slab, anchors, seismic criteria, commodity, storage height, flue spaces, sprinklers, smoke and firefighter access, egress, walls, utilities and permits.

Validate operation

Test empty and loaded entry, placement, extraction, deepest position, top levels, lane status, damaged pallet response, housekeeping, inspection and emergency controls.

Operating discipline inside the rack

Assign each lane and level to an approved load family. Use clear location and status identification, and prevent a truck from entering a lane with incompatible pallets, damaged rails, unstable loads, people, debris or an open work zone. Operators need a defined approach, speed, mast and fork position, placement sequence, withdrawal and damage-reporting process.

Because the truck enters the structure, impacts may occur to columns, rails, brackets, bases and guides. Protect components where design and operation allow, but do not let protection reduce required clearance or create a new snag. Routine observations and competent inspections must cover deep positions that are difficult to see.

Design review questions

Lane assignment

Does the inventory model show enough pallets per SKU, lot and status to fill lanes without frequent mixing or honeycombing?

Pallet support

Do actual pallets bear correctly on both rails at every position without excessive deflection, damage, unstable overhang or product shift?

Truck clearance

Can the actual equipment and load enter, raise, place and withdraw through the whole lane without striking rack or stored pallets?

Fire protection

Have storage height, commodity, plastics, pallets, flue spaces, lane geometry, sprinklers, access and permits been reviewed for the actual design?

Damage response

Can operators see, report and isolate rail, bracket, frame, anchor, guide and pallet damage before another entry?

Change control

Who approves new pallets, loads, SKUs, lane assignments, beam or rail levels, truck models, guides, protection and component repairs?

Information for a drive-in rack brief

Provide pallet and unit-load specifications, samples and condition data; SKU quantity by lot or status; rotation and throughput; lane-depth preferences; lift-truck information; building survey; slab and fire documents; current damage; environment; growth; schedule; and responsible reviewers.

Require proposals to state lane assignment assumptions, usable capacity calculation, exact pallet envelope, rail and configuration basis, truck clearances, fire assumptions, engineering, anchors, protection, installation, inspection, labels, training, permits, maintenance and exclusions.

Prepare a dense-storage brief

Organize pallets, SKU depth, rotation, throughput, equipment, building and fire information before comparing drive-in concepts.

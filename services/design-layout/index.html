Scope before drawings

Warehouse Rack Layout Design Process

A useful rack layout connects verified unit loads, inventory and order profiles, handling equipment, material flow, building conditions, fire protection, approvals, installation, and future change. Pallet count alone is not a design.

Short answer: start with load and operating data, survey the actual facility, protect fixed constraints, compare storage alternatives, coordinate engineering and fire requirements, and issue controlled deliverables. Availability of surveys, engineering, stamped drawings, permitting, or site services must be confirmed in a written project scope; this page does not promise that every service is available in every location.

Inputs that define the layout

Unit loads

Maximum weight and dimensions, pallet or container type, support geometry, stability, overhang, commodity, hazards, rotation, quantity and future changes.

Inventory and orders

SKU count, velocity, lot or expiry rules, reserve quantity, lines per order, handling unit, seasonality, returns, quarantine and peak conditions.

Material flow

Receiving, inspection, reserve, replenishment, picking, consolidation, packing, shipping, WIP, returns, scrap and empty-container paths.

Handling equipment

Lift-truck model, aisle and turning requirements, reach, lowered and raised dimensions, attachments, capacity, charging, carts and pedestrians.

Building

Measured footprint, clear height, columns, slab and joints, doors, docks, walls, stairs, offices, utilities, lights, ducts, slopes and obstructions.

Rules and approvals

Egress, accessibility, fire protection, storage height, commodity, permits, landlord, insurer, security, environmental and operational requirements.

Design sequence

Validate the project brief

Separate verified facts from estimates. Record the source, date, owner and uncertainty for load, building and operating data. Unknowns become action items, not silent assumptions.

Map current and future flow

Trace movement and identify congestion, double handling, excessive replenishment, cross-traffic, blocked staging and growth constraints. Include normal and peak states.

Freeze non-negotiable clear zones

Protect exits, fire equipment, doors, docks, columns, maintenance access, electrical equipment, utilities, work cells, charging, pedestrian routes and approved staging.

Compare alternatives

Test selective, dense, dynamic, long-load, shelving, automation, or mixed concepts against access, throughput, rotation, flexibility, equipment, maintenance, disruption and lifecycle cost.

Coordinate and approve

Move the chosen concept through rack design, structural review, fire protection, building and operational approval as applicable. Record revisions and resolve interfaces.

Compare more than storage positions

MeasureQuestionEvidence
Usable capacityHow many real load positions fit after clearances, incompatibilities and operating reserves?SKU-to-location test using actual load families
FlowHow far and how often do loads, trucks and people move?Travel model, process map and peak simulation
ServiceCan required orders and replenishments be completed at peak?Throughput assumptions, task times and bottleneck review
ChangeabilityWhat happens when products, volumes, equipment or processes change?Scenario tests and controlled expansion options
Risk and maintainabilityCan the system be inspected, protected, cleaned, repaired and operated predictably?Access, inspection, damage, maintenance and emergency review

A conceptual layout is not a construction release. Before purchase or installation, obtain the product-specific design, engineering, anchorage, slab verification, fire-protection coordination, permits, and approvals required for the actual site and scope.

Useful design deliverables

Basis of design

Project objectives, verified loads, data sources, operating assumptions, exclusions, criteria, responsible reviewers, and unresolved items.

Existing-condition plan

Measured building, columns, doors, docks, utilities, fire equipment, obstructions, clear zones, equipment and relevant floor information.

Alternative concepts

Comparable layouts with capacity, flow, equipment, staging, access, risks, changeability, cost scope and assumptions.

Approved layout

Rack rows and types, dimensions, aisles, loads, storage elevations, protection, labels, staging, routes and coordinated interfaces.

Technical package

Product-specific drawings, calculations, details, anchors, reactions, capacity information, specifications and required professional seals.

Handover records

Permits, approved revisions, inspections, as-built information, load signs, training, warranties, maintenance and change-control ownership.

Questions before engaging a designer

Ask who measures the site, validates load data, selects rack products, provides engineering, coordinates sprinklers, supports permits, checks lift-equipment fit, manages revisions, verifies installation, and owns the final record set. Confirm jurisdiction, professional licensing, insurance, deliverable format, schedule, exclusions, and use rights in writing.

Do not assume a “permit-ready” or “engineered” marketing phrase defines a deliverable. Specify whether drawings are conceptual, supplier drawings, calculations, stamped documents, permit submittals, responses to comments, fabrication releases, installation drawings, or as-builts.

Red flags in a layout

Capacity without loads

A position count based on generic pallets can fail when real dimensions, weights, overhang and incompatible products are applied.

Aisles without equipment

An aisle width is not valid until the actual truck, load, turning, reach, guidance, traffic and safety conditions are confirmed.

Drawings without control

If revision, approval, field change and as-built processes are unclear, installation may drift away from the reviewed design.

CARTON FLOW PLANNING GUIDE

Carton Flow Rack Systems

A practical guide to container data, gravity lanes, replenishment, picking, ergonomics, testing and quotation scope.

Original guide by zhengdarack · Updated 21 November 2026

Short answer

A carton flow rack presents cartons or totes at the picking face by letting them travel along inclined rollers or wheels under gravity. It can separate replenishment from picking and support dense forward-pick locations, but only when the container, lane, slope, stop, rack, operating method and maintenance plan work together. Representative product testing is a core selection step.

Where do carton flow rack systems fit?

Carton flow is commonly considered for a forward-picking area where operators repeatedly select individual cartons, totes or pieces. Reserve stock may sit elsewhere or above the lanes. Replenishment normally occurs from the rear while picking occurs from the front, reducing direct interference between the two tasks.

The system is not automatically the best choice for every SKU. It becomes less predictable when containers are damaged, very light, unusually soft, unstable, open-bottomed or inconsistent. Slow-moving stock may not justify a dedicated lane. Products with leakage, contamination or special environmental requirements need their own review.

Build a container and SKU schedule first

The rack frame is only one part of the system. The travelling unit controls lane selection. Record actual minimum and maximum conditions instead of relying on a nominal carton size.

  • Outside length, width and height, with the travel direction identified.
  • Minimum and maximum gross weight.
  • Base material, ribs, seams, feet, straps and areas that may sag.
  • Rigidity when full, partly full and empty.
  • Centre-of-gravity changes and uneven internal loads.
  • Acceptable orientation at replenishment and picking.
  • Temperature, moisture, dust and cleaning conditions.
  • SKU velocity, replenishment unit and desired presentation quantity.

Photographs of the base are useful, but physical samples are stronger evidence. Test the lightest, heaviest and least favourable representative containers, not only the easiest carton.

Avoid a common mistake: the carton that rolls well when new may behave differently after transport, moisture exposure, stacking or repeated use.

How should the gravity lane be selected?

A complete lane includes the rolling surface, supports, entry, end stop, separators and any presentation feature. Its behaviour depends on the combined system rather than one catalogue component.

Roller or wheel arrangement

Full-width rollers can provide broad support. Wheel tracks can be configured into lanes and may suit defined container widths. The correct choice depends on container-base contact, load distribution, size range, adjustability and maintenance access.

Slope and travel

The required slope is project- and product-specific. Too little may allow a unit to stop before reaching the face; too much may increase arrival speed and impact. Test the proposed depth, load range and components in the intended environment.

End stop and presentation

The stop must retain the product without creating unacceptable damage or an awkward pick. A presentation shelf or tilted face may improve visibility and reach for some work, but it changes geometry and should be assessed with the operator, container and replenishment method.

Coordinate replenishment and picking as one process

A gravity lane cannot correct an unclear operating method. Define who replenishes, from which aisle, in what unit, at what frequency and under which stock-rotation rule.

  1. Load only the approved container and orientation into each lane.
  2. Do not force a unit behind a stopped or damaged carton.
  3. Keep lane labels visible from the correct side.
  4. Define what the picker does when a carton fails to advance.
  5. Separate inspection, damaged-product and returned-stock procedures.
  6. Control lane changes so track position and labels remain matched.

If a FIFO sequence is required, the warehouse process must preserve it. Rear loading and front picking support that objective, but mixed loading, manual bypassing or uncontrolled returns can change the sequence.

Plan the pick face around real work

Review the complete reach, lift and repetition pattern rather than choosing a convenient frame height. Heavy or high-frequency items may need different zones from light, slow-moving cartons. Consider the range of expected users, product grip, pick quantity, visibility, lighting and the need to open or break down cartons.

  • Place high-frequency items where the task can be performed without repeated extreme reaches.
  • Provide enough hand clearance at the stop and between levels.
  • Avoid sharp edges and uncontrolled protrusions at the pick face.
  • Keep walking and equipment routes clear of cartons and packaging waste.
  • Review replenishment ergonomics separately from picking ergonomics.

For US workplaces, OSHA's materials-handling rule addresses clear aisles and secure storage. OSHA's warehousing guidance also reminds operators not to exceed rack or shelving capacities. Other locations require their applicable review.

How does carton flow integrate with the rack?

Flow beds may sit in dedicated shelving or within a larger rack structure. Confirm the support points, frame capacity, beam arrangement, connections, anchorage, protection and interaction with reserve storage. Do not assume a flow insert can be added to an existing rack without checking the rack and the revised loading.

Coordinate sprinklers, lighting, conveyors, pick-to-light equipment, labels, dividers and any overhanging reserve pallets. Confirm access for cleaning, inspection, adjustment and component replacement.

For an example of manufacturer-described product use, Interroll states that its Carton Versi Flow is intended for cartons and totes of different sizes and weights. That source illustrates one product family; it does not establish another project's performance or suitability.

Prototype before finalising repeated lanes

A representative trial is especially valuable when many identical lanes will be purchased. Use production containers and test the unfavourable combinations.

  • Load from the intended replenishment position.
  • Test minimum and maximum weight.
  • Observe travel, tracking, arrival and stopping.
  • Remove the front unit and confirm the following unit advances.
  • Repeat after realistic container wear or environmental exposure.
  • Record the approved container, lane setup and adjustment limits.

After installation, train operators to recognise stopped units, damaged rollers, loose tracks, displaced stops and rack damage. Create an inspection frequency based on use and risk, isolate unsafe conditions and use approved replacement parts and procedures.

Carton flow rack systems: common questions

Is carton flow rack the same as pallet flow rack?

No. Both use gravity, but carton flow is normally planned for cartons, totes or small units at picking faces. Pallet flow handles pallets and requires different loads, braking, interfaces and equipment review.

Can every carton run on every roller track?

No. Container base condition, stiffness, size, weight, orientation and load distribution affect travel. Representative samples should be tested in the proposed lane arrangement.

Does carton flow guarantee FIFO rotation?

It can support first-in, first-out presentation when replenishment and picking occur from opposite sides and the operating method is followed. Mixed loading or bypassing the lane can defeat the intended sequence.

How many cartons fit in one lane?

That depends on usable lane depth, container length in the travel direction, required clearances and the approved stop and replenishment arrangement. Do not estimate capacity from frame depth alone.

What should be included in a quotation request?

Send the SKU and container schedule, maximum and minimum weights, base photographs, daily movement, replenishment and picking method, layout, elevations, aisle constraints, destination and required scope.

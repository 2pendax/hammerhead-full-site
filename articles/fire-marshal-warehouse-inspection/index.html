WAREHOUSE FIRE-INSPECTION GUIDE

Fire Marshal Warehouse Inspection

Prepare the real operating warehouse—not a temporary display—by aligning records, storage, fire protection, exits and daily controls.

Original guide by zhengdarack · Updated 21 November 2026

Short answer

A fire marshal or fire-code inspection checks whether the building's actual use and storage remain consistent with applicable approvals and fire-safety requirements. Prepare by confirming the local authority's scope, collecting permits and system records, comparing the approved storage plan with field conditions, and correcting blocked exits, impaired protection, uncontrolled storage height, missing access and hazardous-material issues. Requirements vary by jurisdiction, commodity and fire-protection design.

What type of fire inspection is being performed?

Ask the authority having jurisdiction, often called the AHJ, what triggered the visit and what documents should be available. A periodic operational inspection can differ from a permit inspection, a change-of-use review, a high-piled-storage inspection or a follow-up on a reported condition.

  • Identify the inspector, department and inspection reference.
  • Confirm the building area and operations within scope.
  • Ask whether a responsible facilities person and system contractor should attend.
  • Clarify required permits, plans, tests and previous correction notices.
  • Record the adopted-code and local-amendment references provided by the authority.
Do not use this page as a universal code checklist. Storage height, commodity, packaging, plastics, aerosols, flammable liquids, batteries and sprinkler design can change the requirements substantially.

Which records should be ready?

Create one inspection folder with the current approved information and a simple index. Avoid handing over outdated drawings without explaining their status.

Record groupExamples to confirm
Occupancy and permitsUse approval, storage or high-piled-storage permits, alteration permits and current conditions
Approved layoutRack plan, storage heights, aisles, access routes, commodity zones and exits
Fire protectionSprinkler design basis where available, alarm and suppression inspection or service records

A record does not prove the field condition still matches it. Use the documents to perform a physical comparison before the inspector arrives.

Compare approved storage with actual storage

Walk every aisle, staging area, dock, work cell, office store, yard and temporary overflow location. The highest or most hazardous storage is not always in the main rack.

  • Commodity and packaging match the approved description.
  • Storage height stays within the permitted or designed limit.
  • Rack rows, aisles and storage blocks match the current plan.
  • Flue spaces and other required openings are not bridged by pallets, cartons, shrink wrap or loose stock.
  • Top loads, overhang and unstable stacking do not create hazards.
  • Empty pallets, packaging waste and returns have controlled locations.
  • Temporary and seasonal storage follow the same limits as normal stock.
  • Charging, maintenance and processing areas have not become overflow storage.

Changes in plastic content, container type, pallet type or packaging can alter the fire challenge even when the product name remains the same. Route uncertain commodities to the appropriate fire-protection and code professionals.

Check sprinkler and alarm conditions without guessing the design

Look for visible obstructions, damage, leaks, closed or unsecured valves, missing identification, blocked risers and unreported system changes. Confirm that required inspection, testing and maintenance have been completed by the responsible qualified provider.

OSHA's current warehousing guidance says emergency safeguards such as sprinklers, alarms, fire doors and exit lighting should remain in proper working order. It also points to clear exits and electrical access.

Sprinkler clearance

Do not apply a memorised clearance number to every warehouse. OSHA's general-industry sprinkler rule has an 18-inch provision in certain conditions, and its interpretation on sprinkler spacing explains both the purpose of the clear plane and circumstances where other arrangements may apply. High-rack or high-challenge storage can require more. Use the approved sprinkler design, applicable standard and AHJ direction.

In-rack sprinklers and rack changes

Keep in-rack sprinklers, branch lines and their discharge areas protected from stock and handling damage. Do not move beams, add decks, close flue spaces or change storage height without reviewing the effect on the fire-protection design.

Keep exits and exit routes continuously usable

Inspection-day cleaning is not enough. Exit routes must work during ordinary busy shifts, replenishment and staging.

  • Exit doors open from the inside as required and are not blocked.
  • Exit signs and emergency lighting are visible and operational.
  • Travel paths do not pass through lockable or uncontrolled spaces.
  • Stock, pallets, bins, equipment and charging cables stay outside exit routes.
  • Fire doors are not wedged, obstructed or blocked by stored materials.
  • Aisles remain clear and match the operational and approved layout.

OSHA's exit-route guidance states that exit routes must be free and unobstructed and that emergency safeguards must remain in working order. Local fire and building requirements may add further provisions.

Protect fire-department and equipment access

Inspect the exterior as well as the warehouse interior. Confirm that required fire lanes, hydrants, fire-department connections, gates, key-access arrangements and address identification are visible and accessible. Inside, keep access to risers, alarm panels, extinguishers, hose equipment, electrical panels and shutoffs clear.

Do not hide hazardous-material and special-storage questions

Build an accurate inventory rather than relying on familiar product names. Review quantities, container sizes, physical state, storage method and location. Batteries, aerosols, flammable or combustible liquids, compressed gases, oxidisers and other regulated materials may need specific controls.

  • Match inventory and safety information to the actual stock.
  • Keep incompatible materials separated as required.
  • Verify cabinets, rooms, spill controls and ventilation where applicable.
  • Control damaged, leaking, returned and waste material.
  • Confirm that charging or energy-storage equipment has not been relocated without review.
  • Escalate quantities or products that differ from the approved condition.

Do not move hazardous stock to an unapproved corner simply to clear an aisle.

Run a documented pre-inspection walk

  1. Use the approved plan and previous inspection report as the starting documents.
  2. Walk with operations, facilities, safety and fire-protection responsibilities represented.
  3. Photograph and locate each discrepancy.
  4. Separate immediate housekeeping corrections from items needing design or AHJ clarification.
  5. Assign one owner and due date to each action.
  6. Verify completion in the field, not only by email.
  7. Keep a list of unresolved questions ready for transparent discussion.

A good pre-walk is not a promise that an inspection will pass. It reduces avoidable discrepancies and makes unknowns visible before they become rushed decisions.

How should inspection findings be closed?

Ask for a written report or record of findings. For each item, capture the cited condition, required outcome, due date, evidence needed and reinspection process. If the instruction is unclear, obtain clarification before performing permanent work.

Some corrections are simple housekeeping; others affect sprinklers, racks, exits, hazardous-material controls or permits and require qualified design or contractor input. Do not treat a closed work order as proof until the field condition and required documentation have been verified.

Keep the improvement: add recurring checks for storage height, exits, fire access, sprinkler clearance, flue spaces and temporary staging so the warehouse does not return to the same condition after the inspection.

Fire marshal warehouse inspection questions

What does a fire marshal inspect in a warehouse?

The exact scope varies by jurisdiction and inspection type. Common subjects include approved use and storage, fire-protection systems, exits, aisles, fire-department access, hazardous materials, housekeeping, electrical clearances and required records.

Is 18 inches always the correct sprinkler clearance?

Do not treat one number as universal. OSHA has a general 18-inch requirement for certain sprinklered storage, but sprinkler type, storage arrangement, commodity and adopted fire code can require different or greater clearance. Confirm the approved design and local requirements.

Can storage be moved before an inspection?

Temporary movement can hide the real operating condition and does not create ongoing compliance. Prepare the normal layout and correct the underlying issue.

Will an inspection approve the pallet-rack capacity?

A fire inspection is not a substitute for rack design, load documentation, installation review or structural approval. Keep those responsibilities and records separate.

What should happen after a deficiency notice?

Record each item, assign a responsible person, clarify the required evidence, complete approved corrective work and request reinspection or submit documentation as directed by the authority.

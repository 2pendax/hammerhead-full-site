WAREHOUSE OPERATIONS GUIDE

Warehouse Management Dos and Don'ts

Use simple controls to keep physical stock, system records, people and storage equipment aligned every day.

Original guide by zhengdarack · Updated 22 November 2026

Short answer

Do make every receipt, move, pick and exception visible and traceable. Do verify that loads fit approved storage locations. Do review backlog, accuracy, damage and equipment availability daily. Don't bypass transactions, hide damaged stock, move rack beams without approval, block aisles or let temporary staging become permanent.

Build control into normal work

A warehouse becomes difficult when people need workarounds to complete ordinary tasks. Define the standard path and a clear exception path.

DoDon't
Give every item, load and location a usable identifierDepend on memory or informal names
Record moves at the time they occurBatch-enter transactions at the end of the shift
Define who owns each exceptionLeave problem stock in an unmarked corner
Make rules easy to follow at the point of workHide critical instructions in a long manual
Review causes when the same issue repeatsTreat every repeat as an isolated mistake

Supervisors should be able to see current backlog, blocked work and safety issues without creating a special report.

Receiving: verify before inventory is released

Do

  • Match the delivery to the appointment and purchase information.
  • Count and identify goods before put-away.
  • Check pallet condition, load stability, labels and damage.
  • Separate accepted, held and rejected stock physically and in the system.
  • Record dimensions and weight when master data is uncertain.
  • Keep docks and fire routes clear of unmanaged dwell.

Don't

  • Put unidentified goods into a convenient empty location.
  • Allow damaged pallets into dynamic or automated storage.
  • Mix lots or statuses before release rules are satisfied.
  • Let receiving overflow block travel routes.

Measure arrival-to-release time and exception age separately. A fast average can hide a small number of very old unresolved receipts.

Inventory accuracy: protect every transaction

Inventory accuracy is the agreement among the physical load, its identity, quantity, status and recorded location.

Do

  • Scan or confirm source, item, quantity and destination.
  • Use check digits or clear location labels.
  • Cycle-count by value, velocity, risk and error history.
  • Freeze or control affected locations during investigation.
  • Correct the root cause as well as the quantity.

Don't

  • Move stock without a transaction because the aisle is busy.
  • Create duplicate temporary locations.
  • Adjust inventory without a reason code and review.
  • Count around active moves without controlling them.
Simple test: if a new operator cannot identify a load, its status and its location rule, the process is relying on local knowledge instead of control.

Slotting: place inventory for the work it creates

Slotting should consider demand, cube, weight, handling, compatibility, replenishment and ergonomics.

DoDon't
Use current order and movement dataKeep locations forever because “that is where it has always been”
Separate incompatible or controlled goodsOptimize travel while ignoring product rules
Match load to approved location capacity and supportUse any location that is empty
Reserve enough pick-face capacity for replenishmentLet fast movers create constant emergency replenishment
Review seasonal and promotional changesUse annual averages for peak periods

Re-slotting has a cost. Prioritize changes with measurable travel, congestion, safety or service benefit.

Replenishment: plan before the pick face empties

Do

  • Set minimums from demand during replenishment lead time plus uncertainty.
  • Schedule predictable replenishment outside the busiest picking period where practical.
  • Prioritize stock-out risk and customer cut-offs.
  • Separate reserve and forward-pick location logic.
  • Measure emergency replenishments and missed tasks.

Don't

  • Wait for an operator to discover an empty face.
  • Replenish a location while people are exposed below an active load.
  • Mix batches or statuses to save a trip.
  • Block the aisle with multiple unprocessed loads.

When picking and replenishment share an aisle, define right-of-way, timing and temporary closure rules.

Picking and packing: design out avoidable errors

  • Do present item, location, quantity and verification in a clear sequence.
  • Do separate look-alike items and require additional confirmation where risk is high.
  • Do provide exception paths for shorts, damage and unreadable labels.
  • Do balance work before queues form at packing or consolidation.
  • Don't reward speed without accuracy, damage and safety measures.
  • Don't leave open containers or loose product in travel paths.
  • Don't bypass weight, scan or quality checks to recover schedule.

Measure first-pass completion and error cause. Rework hidden outside the main process makes productivity look better than it is.

Rack and storage management

Do

  • Display accurate load limits and configuration information.
  • Verify pallet dimensions, weight and support before storage.
  • Keep flues, sprinkler clearances and aisles as approved.
  • Report impacts and isolate damaged areas promptly.
  • Use competent inspections and approved repairs.
  • Control layout, beam-level and accessory changes.

Don't

  • Move beams to fit taller product without review.
  • Place heavier loads because the beam “looks strong.”
  • Use damaged pallets or unidentified rack components.
  • Drill, weld, heat or straighten structural rack parts without an approved method.
  • Hide column damage behind a new guard.

The Rack Manufacturers Institute FAQ explains that beam elevation changes outside the original load application and configuration may create an unsafe condition and should be reviewed.

Traffic, people and housekeeping

OSHA's materials-handling rule requires sufficient safe clearances where mechanical equipment is used, clear aisles and passageways, and stable storage.

  • Do separate people and vehicles where practical.
  • Do keep exits, panels, valves and emergency equipment accessible.
  • Do define parking, charging, staging and crossing locations.
  • Do clean spills and remove debris promptly.
  • Don't use marked aisles as overflow storage.
  • Don't accept blind corners without visibility or traffic control.
  • Don't let parked equipment narrow a designed aisle.

Equipment and maintenance control

DoDon't
Plan preventive maintenance around operating demandRun equipment until failure
Keep critical spares and escalation contactsAssume a supplier can deliver immediately
Tag and isolate unsafe equipmentReturn it to service with an informal fix
Record fault, cause, action and verificationClose work when the alarm disappears
Test recovery and fallback proceduresWait for a real outage to learn them

Use a small balanced daily dashboard

Choose measures that lead to action, not a wall of numbers.

  • Inbound and outbound backlog against cut-off.
  • Dock dwell and old exceptions.
  • Inventory discrepancies and blocked locations.
  • Order completion and first-pass accuracy.
  • Emergency replenishments and pick-face stock-outs.
  • Damage, rack impacts and safety events.
  • Equipment availability and open critical maintenance.
  • Labour hours by major process.

Show target, current result, trend, owner and recovery action. Never improve one metric by hiding work in another queue.

A simple management rhythm

  1. Start of shift: review demand, backlog, staffing, equipment and safety constraints.
  2. During shift: manage exceptions at the source and update priorities visibly.
  3. End of shift: hand over open work, holds, damage and equipment status.
  4. Weekly: review recurring causes, inventory accuracy, capacity and maintenance.
  5. Monthly: review slotting, layout, peak outlook, training and improvement results.

When a workaround repeats, treat it as process data. Either redesign the standard or remove the cause.

Warehouse-management questions

What is the most important rule?

Keep physical stock, records and location rules synchronized through disciplined transactions and exception control.

How can inventory accuracy improve?

Use controlled moves, clear identification, risk-based cycle counting and rapid root-cause correction.

Should every empty rack location be filled?

No. The load must match the approved capacity, dimensions, support, fire arrangement and inventory rules.

Can operators move rack beams?

Not without approval; elevation changes can affect capacity, clearances and sprinkler relationships.

Which metrics should be daily?

Backlog, dwell, inventory exceptions, completion, damage, blocked locations and equipment availability are a useful balanced set.

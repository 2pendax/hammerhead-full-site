RECEIVING-TO-SHIPPING FLOW

Distribution Center Racking

Design reserve, replenishment and picking storage as part of one peak-throughput system—not as isolated pallet positions.

Original industry guide by zhengdarack · Updated 22 November 2026

Design principle: the best rack layout is not the one with the most theoretical positions. It is the layout that holds required inventory while keeping receiving, replenishment, picking and shipping stable at peak demand.

Profile inventory and flow together

Use time-based data, not only SKU totals.

DataDesign use
Inventory by SKU and day/weekReserve capacity, lane depth and seasonality
Receipts by hour and loadDocks, staging and put-away demand
Orders by line, case and palletPicking method and outbound work
Replenishment movesForward-pick sizing and aisle conflict
Load dimensions and weightRack, support and truck compatibility
Service cut-offsPeak labour, staging and carrier flow
Returns and exceptionsControlled status and processing space

Separate normal, peak and stress scenarios. The average day can hide the exact hour that determines docks, forklifts and staging.

Use storage zones for different work

Selective reserve

Direct access and flexibility for varied SKUs and uncertain inventory.

Dense reserve

Deep-lane, flow or shuttle storage for compatible high-quantity product families.

Forward picking

Case, carton or pallet pick faces sized for demand during replenishment lead time.

Exception zones

Returns, damage, quarantine, oversize goods and unidentified inventory with controlled status.

Automation can support repetitive high-volume routes, but controls, interfaces, maintenance, recovery and system availability need a complete business case.

Design from the docks inward

Receiving and shipping are not leftover space. Calculate staging from appointment pattern, processing time, route waves and dwell.

  • Separate unloaded, received, held and put-away-ready goods.
  • Keep door approaches and fire routes clear.
  • Provide cross-dock paths for eligible product.
  • Avoid putting long-term storage in front of active doors.
  • Plan empty pallets, returns, waste and packaging.
  • Model simultaneous inbound and outbound peaks.

When dock staging fills, loads migrate into travel aisles and create congestion even if rack capacity remains available.

Control reserve-to-pick replenishment

Forward locations should cover expected demand through replenishment response time plus uncertainty. Too small creates emergency moves; too large consumes prime pick space.

DoAvoid
Prioritize by stock-out risk and order cut-offWaiting for the picker to find an empty face
Schedule predictable work outside pick peaksUncontrolled replenishment in occupied aisles
Confirm lot, status and quantityMixing product to save a trip
Measure emergency replenishmentsHiding the task as general forklift travel

Where picking and replenishment share an aisle, define right-of-way and temporary closure rules.

Slot for total work, not velocity alone

  • Order frequency and quantity.
  • Cube, weight and ergonomic handling.
  • SKU affinity and order path.
  • Replenishment volume and source distance.
  • Product compatibility and security.
  • Seasonal and promotional change.
  • Damage and contamination risk.

A very fast SKU can create congestion if too many workers and replenishment moves converge on one location. Provide enough faces or distribute demand where practical.

Size forklifts, aisles and crossings

Use actual truck, mast, attachment and load data. Include rack guards, pallet overhang, turns, columns, doors and passing needs.

  • Separate pedestrians from vehicle travel where practical.
  • Mark and control cross-aisle intersections.
  • Provide parking and charging outside active travel.
  • Model queues at docks and high-volume pick zones.
  • Keep exits, panels, valves and emergency equipment accessible.

Do not narrow an aisle to gain positions without checking the complete manoeuvre and operating risk.

Measure usable capacity by zone

MeasureMeaning
DesignedApproved physical positions
CompatiblePositions suitable for the load family
UsableCompatible positions after lane, batch and buffer rules
OccupiedPositions holding inventory
AvailableUsable positions free and not blocked

Review utilization by zone and product family. One full compatible zone can stop receiving while the building still appears partly empty.

Protect configuration and condition

  • Verify loads, beam levels and load signs.
  • Control beam moves and component substitutions.
  • Report impacts and isolate damaged areas.
  • Use guards suited to actual traffic hazards.
  • Maintain flues, sprinkler clearance and top-of-storage limits.
  • Inspect rack, anchors, decks and pallets.
  • Review repeated findings by location and cause.

High throughput increases exposure. Impact data should lead to layout, truck, protection or training changes—not only repeated repairs.

Validate the operating design

  1. Fit peak inventory by compatible zone.
  2. Simulate inbound and outbound peak hours.
  3. Check replenishment against picking demand.
  4. Trace forklift and pedestrian conflicts.
  5. Test dock and staging dwell.
  6. Run equipment-unavailable and blocked-aisle scenarios.
  7. Confirm fire, structural and permit requirements.
  8. Define growth triggers before service declines.

Distribution racking questions

Which rack is best?

Most centers use a mix based on SKU quantity, access, rotation and throughput.

How is capacity measured?

Separate designed, compatible, usable, occupied and available positions by zone.

How should fast movers be slotted?

Use velocity, cube, weight, replenishment, ergonomics and congestion together.

Why can the center fail before storage is full?

Docks, staging, cross-aisles, replenishment, packing, labour or one storage zone may become the first bottleneck.

INDUSTRIAL PROPERTY AND LEASING

Commercial Real Estate Warehouse Racking

Connect storage feasibility with clear height, slab, fire protection, lease responsibility, approvals and exit obligations.

Original industry guide by zhengdarack · Updated 22 November 2026

Property rule: advertised square footage and clear height do not prove usable storage capacity. A feasibility layout must account for columns, roof slope, sprinklers, exits, docks, staging, forklifts, rack design and actual inventory.

Complete storage due diligence before commitment

Evaluate the building and transaction together. Important questions include:

  • Is the proposed warehouse or distribution use permitted?
  • What storage height and commodity can the fire system support?
  • Where is clear height measured, and what reduces it?
  • Is reliable slab information available for rack and equipment?
  • Do columns, doors and docks support efficient flow?
  • Are exits, fire access and emergency systems compatible with the plan?
  • Who pays for design, permits, upgrades and restoration?
  • Can the project be approved and built before occupancy is needed?

Record open conditions in the lease or acquisition decision rather than assuming they will be resolved later.

Use a current site survey. Marketing plans and previous-tenant drawings may be incomplete or outdated.

Translate inventory into building demand

Storage demand

Peak pallets by size, weight, SKU, batch, rotation and compatible location family.

Flow demand

Inbound, outbound, replenishment, picking, returns and daily peaks.

Handling demand

Forklift types, aisles, charging, maintenance, parking and recovery.

Support demand

Staging, packing, offices, waste, quality hold, security and growth.

Compare buildings using usable operational capacity and throughput, not only annual rent per square foot. A less expensive property can require more labour or upgrades.

Lease language needs legal review. The technical team should provide clear descriptions of physical work and evidence needed for acceptance.

Evaluate existing racking as an asset, not a free bonus

Existing rack may save time only when it can be identified, inspected and approved for the tenant's loads and configuration.

  • Collect manufacturer, model, drawings and capacity documents.
  • Inspect columns, braces, beams, connectors, bases and anchors.
  • Compare beam elevations and row layout with records.
  • Verify compatibility of decks, guards and accessories.
  • Review slab, seismic and fire conditions for the new use.
  • Identify missing or mixed components and prior repairs.
  • Budget corrections, signs and permit revisions.

Do not rely on the previous occupant's load signs if the configuration or loads have changed.

Plan approvals and tenant improvements

The warehouse plan may involve use, building, rack, mezzanine, fire, electrical, mechanical or operational permits. Begin during feasibility and assign a coordinator.

  • Coordinate rack layout with exits, sprinklers and utilities.
  • Freeze commodity and storage-height assumptions.
  • Issue one revision-controlled plan across disciplines.
  • Allow time for comments, redesign and inspections.
  • Do not order equipment solely from a conceptual test fit.

Landlord approval is not a government permit, and permit approval does not allocate lease cost.

Installation in a leased facility

  • Document existing conditions before work.
  • Confirm delivery, staging and working-hour restrictions.
  • Scan or clear anchor locations as required.
  • Protect walls, doors, floors and existing services.
  • Keep exits and fire systems available during phasing.
  • Retain approved drawings, installation and inspection records.
  • Transfer operating limits and maintenance duties to the tenant team.

Record every approved deviation. Uncontrolled field changes create problems at inspection, insurance review and lease exit.

Exit and restoration planning

Define the end before installation begins.

  1. Identify which assets are tenant-owned, landlord-owned or removable.
  2. Define decommissioning, unloading and safe dismantling.
  3. Specify anchor removal or treatment and slab-repair standard.
  4. Address fire, electrical and utility changes.
  5. Document waste, resale or transfer of components.
  6. Inspect restored areas and obtain written acceptance.
  7. Retain final records for claims and future use.

Do not cut rack at the floor or abandon anchors without an approved method and property acceptance.

Commercial property questions

Should rack be designed before lease signing?

At least complete feasibility and identify structural, fire, permit, access and utility risks first.

Is existing rack part of the building?

Ownership and responsibility depend on the transaction, lease and local law; verify documents and condition.

What building data is needed?

A current survey, clear heights, columns, overhead services, slab, docks, exits, sprinklers and permitted use.

Who repairs anchor holes?

The lease and project scope should assign removal, repair standard, inspection and acceptance.

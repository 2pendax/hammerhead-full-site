Multi Tier Racking

Multi tier racking creates two or more accessible storage levels within a high warehouse, usually for cartons, bins and piece picking. A successful system connects storage geometry with safe movement of people and goods rather than simply stacking more shelves vertically.

The short answer

Multi tier racking is a multi-level storage system in which shelving or rack structures, walkways and floors create accessible tiers for manual picking and replenishment. It is often used for spare parts, cartons, bins, apparel, records or e-commerce items. Buyers should define the item profile, shelf and floor loads, number of tiers, usable headroom, stairs, conveyors or lifts, replenishment method, escape routes, fire-protection interfaces, supporting-floor conditions and future change process before requesting comparable proposals.

What is multi tier racking?

Multi tier racking uses warehouse height to create multiple working and storage levels. Shelving or rack uprights may support walkways and floors, or the levels may combine independent structural support with storage equipment. People normally access goods on each tier while conveyors, lifts or controlled transfer openings move cartons and totes between levels.

A shelving-supported mezzanine floor is one way to create levels. “Multi tier” describes the operating arrangement; the final support concept must be stated in the proposal.

When does a multi-level storage system make sense?

  • The warehouse has usable height but limited floor area.
  • Goods are suitable for manual or assisted piece and carton handling.
  • SKU quantity and pick activity justify organized locations across tiers.
  • Replenishment can be scheduled without blocking picking routes.
  • Stairs, transfers, fire protection and escape routes can be coordinated.

Multi-tier storage may be a poor fit for mostly palletized goods, frequent forklift access to every location, very heavy or irregular items, low clear height or constantly changing layouts. Compare lifecycle handling effort—not only the number of storage positions.

Test the slotting plan against a normal day and a peak period. A theoretical capacity gain can be lost if every order requires repeated stair or lift movements.

Which loads must be specified for multi tier racking?

  • Maximum item, shelf, bay and run loads on every tier.
  • Goods stored directly on walkways or floor zones.
  • Shelving feet and other concentrated reactions.
  • People, carts, workstations and packing equipment.
  • Wheel loads, spacing and approved cart routes.
  • Conveyors, lifts, stairs, guardrails and services.

Use a level-by-level load plan. Do not assume identical capacities when geometry, use or support conditions differ between tiers.

How should stairs, conveyors and lifts be selected?

Separate the movement of people from the movement of goods. Stairs serve personnel and emergency travel; conveyors or goods lifts can reduce carrying and improve replenishment.

Which safety and egress details belong in the first layout?

  • Stairs, landings and unobstructed travel routes.
  • Guardrails, kick plates or other falling-object controls.
  • Controlled gates at every transfer opening.
  • Lighting, signs and clear identification by tier and aisle.
  • Inspection access to shelving, floors, connections and services.
  • Emergency arrangements coordinated with occupancy and local requirements.

For covered US workplaces, OSHA 29 CFR 1910.28, 1910.29 and 1910.25 provide starting references for fall protection, guardrails and stairs. Confirm the complete project-specific code and approval basis.

What building information should be reviewed?

  1. Measure clear height, columns, doors, roof obstructions and services.
  2. Verify supporting-floor and base-location information.
  3. Coordinate sprinkler, detection and other fire-protection requirements across tiers.
  4. Coordinate lighting, power, ventilation, data and conveyor routes.
  5. Define occupancy, exits and approval responsibilities.
  6. Reserve installation and maintenance access.

Every additional tier creates new obstructions and occupied routes. Fire protection and egress must be developed with the storage layout, not appended after fabrication.

What should a multi tier racking proposal include?

  • Plans, elevations and sections for every tier.
  • Location counts, opening sizes and item assumptions.
  • Shelf, bay, floor-zone and concentrated-load criteria.
  • Rack/shelving, floors, walkways, connections, bases and anchors.
  • Stairs, rails, gates, conveyors, lifts and protection.
  • Building and fire-protection work by others.
  • Engineering, approval, freight, installation and inspection scope.
  • Final drawings, labels, operating limits and maintenance guidance.

Compare proposals against one inventory, throughput and load brief. A higher location count is not equivalent if opening sizes, access equipment or service scope differ.

How should expansion and later changes be controlled?

Document possible expansion directions, but design the current system for its approved use. Moving shelves, changing loads, adding tiers, relocating stairs or cutting conveyor openings requires coordinated review.

Change rule: if an alteration affects a tier, load, route, edge, connection or building service, update the system drawings and operating information.

Keep final component identification, drawings, load notices, inspection records and approved revisions. Do not remove bracing or mix unidentified parts to create new openings.

Multi tier racking: common questions

How many tiers can a warehouse use?

The practical number depends on usable building height, level depths, headroom, loads, access, transfers, fire protection, egress and project approvals.

Is multi tier racking only for shelving?

It is most often associated with manual shelving and picking, but the support and storage equipment must be defined for the actual proposal.

Can forklifts operate on upper tiers?

Do not assume so. Upper-level vehicles create different loads, edges, access and operating requirements that need specific design.

Can the system be expanded later?

Potentially, when the new layout, loads, stability, supporting floor, access, services and approvals are reviewed.

What makes a useful quotation brief?

Provide inventory dimensions, weights, quantities and velocity, location counts, tier uses and loads, replenishment and picking flow, transfer equipment, building and floor data, destination and required approval scope.

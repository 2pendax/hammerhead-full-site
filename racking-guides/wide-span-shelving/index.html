Wide Span Shelving

Wide span shelving can organise bulky hand-picked goods across a warehouse zone. Plan bay locations, picking routes and replenishment together.

Published by zhengdarack · Updated 16 November 2026

Plan Your Rack Specification →
The short answer

Wide span shelving is hand-loaded storage with broad shelf openings for cartons, bins and bulky goods. In a picking area, decide which SKUs use it, how locations are labelled, how reserve stock replenishes each shelf and where completed picks travel. A good single bay can still perform poorly if the aisle layout and refill process are unclear.

Which goods should go into a wide span shelving zone?

Group goods by how they are picked and replenished, not by physical size alone. Start with items too bulky for small-bin shelving but still intended for hand loading. Keep forklift-loaded pallets in a separately planned system.

Interlake Mecalux describes wide-span shelving as hand-loaded storage for varied product types. Use that as category background; determine the actual allocation from your own order and load information.

How should picking and replenishment move through the zone?

Show the product path from reserve stock to the shelf, then from the shelf to order checking. Mark where containers wait and whether refill work shares the picking aisle.

Trial a refill while a normal pick occurs. If the same aisle cannot support both tasks as planned, adjust the schedule, route or layout. Do not evaluate the shelves only while the area is empty.

How do you assign locations across wide shelving bays?

Give every usable position an identifier that maps to the stock record. A simple local code can identify zone, aisle, bay, level and position. Explain the code to the users and keep labels visible with full containers in place.

  1. Mark the physical locations before assigning goods.
  2. Allocate representative stock using normal and peak quantities.
  3. Check similar-looking items and required batch separation.
  4. Define locations for returns, empties and unidentified goods.
  5. Record moves before relying on the new location for picking.

Keep frequently changing items from forcing undocumented shelf alterations. When a new carton exceeds the approved size or load, review the location and rack configuration before using it.

What rack information should accompany the layout?

The zone plan should link each bay type to a specification. Record clear width, clear height, supported depth, shelf surface, maximum level load and complete bay limit.

For detailed measurement and component questions, use the longspan shelving dimensions guide. This page focuses on the repeated layout and work process rather than duplicating those component details.

Illustrative location count

Eight bays with four shelf levels and three defined locations per level contain 8 × 4 × 3 = 96 planned locations. That does not prove the goods fit, establish capacity or account for locations reserved for exceptions.

Keep the location count, stock allocation and rack drawing together. If a column removes one position or a large carton consumes two locations, update the usable count rather than retaining the nominal total.

What should you test before repeating the layout?

  • Fit: largest cartons enter and leave the clear opening.
  • Access: pickers identify, grip and withdraw representative goods.
  • Route: normal picks follow the proposed sequence without unexplained detours.
  • Refill: reserve stock reaches locations with controlled waiting space.
  • Records: physical labels and inventory locations remain aligned.

Record the trial conditions and observations. Do not claim a productivity percentage unless the methods and measured results support it. For the complete shelving workflow, see the warehouse shelving planning guide.

Wide span shelving: common questions

Is wide span shelving different from longspan shelving?

The names are often used for similar wide-opening, hand-loaded products. This guide focuses on the picking-zone layout; the linked longspan guide focuses on bay dimensions and construction.

Should fast-moving items always go at the aisle entrance?

Use actual order relationships, congestion and replenishment needs. A simple popularity rule may miss items commonly picked together or a refill conflict.

Can forklifts replenish wide span shelving?

Do not assume so. Define the load, rack system and equipment interaction, and obtain documentation for the intended operation.

How many locations fit in one bay?

It depends on clear openings, shelf levels, container sizes and the location policy. Count usable assigned positions on the final drawing.

What should a layout proposal include?

Ask for bay types, location counts, clear openings, rated configurations, routes, replenishment assumptions, accessories and project scope.

Multi Tier Shelving for Order Picking

Multi tier shelving for order picking uses building height to create more accessible pick faces, but every extra level adds movement, structural, fire, evacuation and supervision requirements. The design must balance storage density with how people, totes, replenishment stock and completed orders travel between tiers during normal and peak operation.

Published by zhengdarack · Updated 25 November 2026

The short answer

Plan multi tier shelving for order picking by profiling SKU dimensions, weight, velocity, stock cover and handling constraints, then profiling order lines, units, waves and peak hour. Decide which SKUs belong on each tier using velocity, replenishment, affinity, weight and emergency access—not available floor area alone. Define separate routes and capacities for people, pick carts, totes, reserve replenishment, completed orders, waste and returns. Size stairs, lifts, conveyors, transfer points and buffers from simultaneous peak demand, including outage recovery. Specify shelf and platform loads, columns, bracing, connections, foundations or slab, guardrails, gates and impact protection. Coordinate sprinkler coverage, smoke and fire strategy, compartmentation where required, travel distance, exit quantity and width, emergency lighting and responder access with qualified local specialists. Validate congestion, ergonomics, replenishment, evacuation and system failure before commissioning locations, scanning, operating limits, inspections and change control.

A multi-tier system is a vertical process

The tiers do not operate independently. Work released on one level consumes capacity at stairs, lifts, conveyors, packing and dispatch. Design the complete vertical network.

How should SKUs be assigned to each tier?

  • Keep the highest-velocity or heaviest hand-loaded items on the most accessible suitable level.
  • Place slow, light and stable products on upper tiers when travel and replenishment remain acceptable.
  • Group order-affinity items only where transfer and congestion improve.
  • Keep special-security or controlled products in approved zones.
  • Limit oversize and awkward goods to locations with suitable routes and handling.
  • Reserve capacity for seasonal re-slotting without blocking exits or buffers.
  • Rebalance tiers from measured work, not equal SKU counts.

Do not use a goods-movement calculation as proof of evacuation adequacy. Life-safety routes require separate qualified review.

Reserve stock and tier replenishment

  1. Size each forward pick face for demand between reliable replenishments.
  2. Define the reserve source and route for every location.
  3. Schedule routine replenishment away from critical pick waves where possible.
  4. Provide controlled transfer buffers without narrowing travel or escape routes.
  5. Limit tote, carton and pallet loads at every lift and platform interface.
  6. Define emergency replenishment and stock discrepancy procedures.
  7. Measure refill delays and adjust slotting or capacity under change control.

Order release, zoning and consolidation

  • Discrete picking is simple but may create repeated vertical travel.
  • Zone picking keeps operators on tiers but requires reliable tote or order handoff.
  • Batch picking can reduce travel but creates sortation and consolidation work.
  • Wave release can align carrier cutoffs but may overload lifts or packing.
  • Waveless release can smooth work when controls see real capacity.
  • Every container needs identity, destination and exception status through transfers.
  • Consolidation must detect missing tier contributions before packing.

Shelving, reach and ergonomic controls

  • Set shelf depth and height from actual item size, weight and pick frequency.
  • Keep frequent work within comfortable reach zones where practical.
  • Provide approved steps or access equipment; prohibit climbing shelving.
  • Use dividers, bins, lips or mesh to prevent mixing and falling goods.
  • Control tote and carton weight through the full route.
  • Provide lighting, label contrast and scanner access on every tier.
  • Include restocking, cleaning and maintenance tasks in the ergonomic review.

Loads, stability and building interfaces

  • Define shelf, bay, platform, walkway and concentrated equipment loads.
  • Verify columns, beams, bracing, connections, baseplates and anchors.
  • Provide reactions for slab or foundation verification.
  • Check deflection, vibration and movement at conveyors and transfers.
  • Coordinate columns, doors, services, lighting and existing structure.
  • Install guardrails, toe protection, self-closing gates and protected openings.
  • Post loads and inspect damage, looseness, missing parts and unauthorized changes.

Fire protection, smoke strategy and escape

Multi-level storage can change sprinkler obstruction, fire load, smoke movement, travel distance and responder access. Coordinate early with qualified fire, building and local approval specialists.

  • Confirm how shelves, decks and walkways affect sprinkler coverage.
  • Maintain required flues, openings and storage clearances.
  • Define exit number, location, width and protected travel route.
  • Provide emergency lighting, signs, alarms and communication.
  • Keep stairs, landings and routes free of stock and transfer queues.
  • Plan evacuation for all expected users and maintenance conditions.
  • Do not modify decks, enclosures or stored commodities without review.

Multi-tier planning workflow

  1. Profile SKUs, inventory, orders, replenishment and peaks.
  2. Map people, goods, containers, waste and exception flows.
  3. Assign SKUs and workload to candidate tiers.
  4. Size shelves, aisles, buffers and vertical links.
  5. Model tier balance, consolidation and downstream capacity.
  6. Coordinate structure, floor, equipment, fire and egress.
  7. Review ergonomics, guarding and maintenance access.
  8. Install, inspect and commission loads, locations and controls.
  9. Run peak and outage tests before full release.
  10. Monitor workload and change only through controlled review.

Multi tier shelving for order picking: common questions

When is multi-tier shelving useful?

It can add accessible pick faces where building height, SKU count and workload justify multiple levels and the structure, fire, egress and vertical transport can be approved.

Which SKUs should go on upper tiers?

Often lighter, slower or less frequently replenished products, but assignment should follow measured workload, route capacity, ergonomics and emergency planning.

Are conveyors required?

No. Goods lifts, carts or other methods may be used, but every option must be sized for peak flow and have safe guarding, transfer and outage procedures.

Can workers use goods lifts during evacuation?

Do not assume so. The approved fire and evacuation strategy must define independent safe routes and any permitted lift use.

How is multi-tier performance measured?

Track lines per labor hour, travel, replenishment delay, tier and transfer queues, accuracy, downtime, damage and service failures—not storage count alone.

Information to send for a multi-tier picking plan

Send SKU dimensions and weights, velocity and stock cover, orders by hour and season, pick and replenishment methods, container sizes and loads, staffing, equipment and proposed vertical transport, building height and grid, floor or foundation data, fire protection, exits, utilities, local approval requirements and growth scenarios.

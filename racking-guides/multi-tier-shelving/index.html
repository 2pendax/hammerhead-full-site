Multi Tier Shelving

Multi tier shelving organizes cartons, bins and individual items across two or more accessible levels. The best layout begins with SKU dimensions, pick frequency and replenishment flow—not with a generic count of shelves or floor area.

The short answer

Multi tier shelving is a multi-level manual storage arrangement that places shelving, walkways and access equipment on two or more tiers. It is suited to organized storage and picking of cartons, bins, spare parts, apparel, records and similar items. A buyer should provide SKU dimensions and weights, quantities and pick velocity, shelf opening and load requirements, tier assignments, replenishment routes, carts, stairs, conveyors or lifts, fire and egress interfaces, floor information and required engineering and installation scope.

What is multi tier shelving?

Multi tier shelving creates accessible shelving rows on stacked working levels. Workers pick from shelf openings on each tier; goods move between levels by conveyors, goods lifts or controlled transfer points. The shelving may support walkways and floors, or it may sit on independently supported levels.

Allow practical hand clearance and labels, not just the product envelope. A location that technically fits a carton may still be slow or unsafe to pick.

How should products be slotted across levels?

  1. Place high-visit items close to efficient entry, pick and packing routes.
  2. Reduce vertical handling of heavier or awkward containers.
  3. Group order-affinity items where it shortens complete-order travel.
  4. Keep reserve quantities near the locations they replenish.
  5. Separate controlled or incompatible goods where required.
  6. Test the plan with a representative order sample and peak volume.

Re-slotting is normal as demand changes. Preserve location flexibility without assuming structural shelves, walkways or openings can be altered without review.

Which loads should a multi tier shelving proposal state?

  • Maximum load per shelf, bay and shelving run.
  • Floor or walkway load zones on each tier.
  • Shelving-leg and workstation reactions.
  • Cart total weight, wheel loads, spacing and routes.
  • People, packing benches and temporary accumulation.
  • Conveyors, lifts, stairs, rails, gates and services.

Ask the supplier to state the assumptions on drawings and load notices. “Heavy duty shelving” does not replace project-specific shelf, bay and floor criteria.

Calculate replenishment workload, not only picking demand. Small pick faces may save shelf space but create frequent trips that overwhelm one conveyor or lift.

What makes a multi-level pick face practical?

  • Frequently picked goods positioned within comfortable reach where practicable.
  • Clear labels and location codes readable from the approach.
  • Aisle widths matched to people, carts and passing needs.
  • Lighting that reaches shelf openings without glare or deep shadows.
  • Defined temporary space for totes without blocking routes.
  • Consistent shelf modules where operationally useful.

Trial a representative bay with real containers, labels and carts before a large rollout. A short practical test can expose reach, visibility and replenishment problems that drawings do not show.

Which access, fire and egress details require early review?

Show stairs, landings, guardrails, transfer gates, falling-object controls and escape routes before shelf rows are fixed. Coordinate sprinkler and detection needs, lighting, electrical work and penetrations across every tier.

For covered US workplaces, OSHA 29 CFR 1910.28, 1910.29 and 1910.25 provide starting references for fall protection, guardrails and stairs. The project team must confirm the complete local code and approval basis.

What should a multi tier shelving quotation include?

  • Plans, elevations and location schedules for every tier.
  • Shelf opening sizes, quantities and adjustment increments.
  • Shelf, bay, run, floor-zone and concentrated-load assumptions.
  • Shelving, floors, walkways, connections, bases and anchors.
  • Stairs, rails, gates, conveyors, lifts, labels and protection.
  • Fire-protection, electrical and building work boundaries.
  • Engineering, approvals, freight, installation and inspection scope.
  • Final drawings, operating limits and maintenance information.

Compare location counts only when opening sizes and load criteria match. Ask suppliers to separate optional transfer equipment and future expansion so its operational and commercial effect stays visible.

How should future re-slotting and structural changes be controlled?

Moving adjustable shelves within an approved range is different from changing structural bays, floor openings, stairs or supported loads. Define which routine changes operators may make and which require supplier or engineering review.

Keep two records: a live location/slotting plan for inventory and an approved structural configuration for the shelving and levels.

Inspect damage, keep aisles clear and update labels when capacities or uses change. Do not remove bracing or force-fit unidentified parts.

Multi tier shelving: common questions

What products suit multi tier shelving?

It commonly suits cartons, bins, spare parts, apparel, records and other items that people can pick using the approved handling method.

Is it the same as a mezzanine?

Multi-tier shelving describes a storage and picking arrangement. Its floors may be shelving-supported or independently supported, so the proposal must identify the structure.

How many tiers should be used?

The answer depends on building height, headroom, inventory, loads, access, transfers, fire protection, egress and approvals.

Can shelf openings be changed later?

Adjustable shelves may be repositioned within the documented system limits, but structural or load changes require review.

What information improves a quote?

Send SKU dimensions and weights, quantities and velocity, required openings and loads, tier assignments, replenishment and picking process, carts and transfers, building and floor data, destination and approval scope.
